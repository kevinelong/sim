Brine shrimp Artemia

Move the mouse: they swim toward the light.
What am I looking at?

The animal

Artemia, the brine shrimp, is a centimetre-long crustacean of hypersaline lakes. The freeze-dried eggs sold as "Sea-Monkeys" are the same animal. It is nearly transparent, with a pair of dark red compound eyes and a long slender abdomen trailing behind it.

It swims upside down: the ventral side, carrying about eleven pairs of leaf-like legs called phyllopodia, faces up. Those legs beat in a metachronal wave - each pair slightly behind its neighbour in phase, so a ripple runs down the body. That wave both drives the animal forward and sweeps food particles toward its mouth.

Each trunk segment carries a pair of these limbs, one left and one right, both hanging from the underside. The default picture is a side view: the near row is drawn crisp and the far row faintly through the translucent body, which is why a real animal reads as a dense brush rather than a single comb. Switch on top view to look down on them instead - the way the tank in the Pittsburgh experiment was filmed - and the two rows splay to opposite sides of the body axis.

The flow

Around each shrimp you can see a pair of counter-rotating eddies: one off the head, one off the tail. That vortex pair is the two-dimensional signature of the stroke. Far from the animal it is exactly a force dipole, or stresslet - the same idealised object the Pittsburgh team used in place of a real shrimp.

That is why this matters: the paper found that the orientation of such a dipole, relative to the stretching direction of the surrounding flow, decides whether it pushes energy toward larger scales or smaller ones. A swarm of these animals is active matter - a fluid that drives itself.

The paper: X. Si and L. Fang, "Biologically generated turbulent energy flux in shear flow depends on tensor geometry", PNAS Nexus 3(2), pgae056, 2024 - doi, preprint arXiv:2310.03226. The popular account the two views were read against: S. Ornes, "Sea Monkeys Show Scientists How To Rewrite a Rule of Turbulence", Quanta, 2 Oct 2026 - link.

Things to try

1. Sweep the mouse. The shrimp steer toward it and roll to keep their belly, and their beating legs, facing the light.

2. Add a current. Watch the swarm turn to face upstream and the dye stretch into long filaments.

3. Turn the turbulence vortices up and the shrimp down: the dye is then organised by the water alone.

4. Turn the shrimp up and the vortices to zero: everything you see is stirred by animals.

5. Raise leg beat and watch each animal's wake tighten into a faster, narrower jet.
